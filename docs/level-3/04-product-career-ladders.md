# Building Product Career Ladders

Meridian never had a written product career ladder — PMs got promoted when
Amara noticed they deserved it, which worked at eight PMs and stopped
working at eighteen. Priya asks Amara directly in a 1:1: "What does it
actually take to go from PM to Senior PM here, and why did Dana get there
faster than I did?" Amara doesn't have a good answer, because there isn't
one written down — every past promotion was a judgment call, and judgment
calls that aren't documented look like favoritism even when they aren't.
Building the ladder is Amara's job now, not HR's: only she knows what
"good" looks like at each level in a logistics product org.

## Anatomy of a ladder level

A usable level definition has four parts. Skipping any one of them is what
makes most career ladders useless in practice — vague enough that everyone
can convince themselves they already qualify.

| Component | What it answers | Bad example | Good example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | What size/complexity of problem this level owns | "Handles bigger projects" | "Owns a single squad's roadmap for a quarter, unsupervised" |
| Autonomy | How much judgment is exercised without checking in | "More independent" | "Sets their own prioritization criteria; escalates only irreversible calls" |
| Influence | Who they move without formal authority | "Good at cross-functional work" | "Gets two peer PMs to change their roadmap sequencing without Lead intervention" |
| Evidence | What a promotion committee can point to, not just vibes | "They're ready" | "Shipped X, which moved metric Y by Z%, sustained over two quarters" |

## Meridian's product ladder, PM through Product Lead

| Level | Scope | Autonomy | Influence | Typical tenure to next level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PM I | One well-scoped feature area within a squad | Rung 1-2 delegation (see Module 1) | Within their own squad | 12-18 months |
| PM II | A full squad's backlog, one quarter at a time | Rung 3 default | Across 2-3 squads on shared dependencies | 18-24 months |
| Senior PM | A cross-squad initiative or a whole product surface | Rung 4 default | Shapes another PM's roadmap through influence, not authority | 24+ months, or lateral ceiling |
| Product Lead | A pod of 3-5 PMs, a product line's health | Rung 4-5, owns portfolio tradeoffs within their line | Represents their line to the CEO/board without a VP in the room | Varies — role change, not just seniority |

The tenure ranges are guidance, not a formula — a PM who hits every
scope/autonomy/influence bar in 10 months should not wait 12 for a
calendar reason, and Amara says this explicitly in the ladder doc to
prevent it from calcifying into a rule.

## Calibrating without a formula: the evidence packet

Instead of a point-scoring rubric (which invites gaming), Meridian's
promotion process asks each candidate's Lead to assemble an evidence packet
against the four components above, then reviews it in a cross-Lead
calibration session — so no single Lead's standards silently drift looser
or tighter than the others.

| Section | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Scope evidence | The actual scope owned in the last 2 quarters, with a peer-level comparison |
| Autonomy evidence | 2-3 decisions made without escalation, and what rung they operated at |
| Influence evidence | A specific instance of moving another team/PM's plan without formal authority |
| Growth trajectory | What changed between this cycle and the last one — not just a snapshot |
| Gaps | Named explicitly by the sponsoring Lead — a packet with no gaps section is rejected as unvetted |

## Worked example: Priya vs. Dana

Priya's question deserves a real answer. Amara pulls both evidence packets
from the last cycle:

**Dana** (promoted PM I → PM II after 13 months): scope evidence showed she
took over Internal Tools' forecasting feature solo after a teammate left,
scaling it from a 2-person to her owning it alone. Autonomy evidence showed
three rung-3 decisions on scope cuts, escalated correctly when a vendor
contract risk came up. Influence evidence showed her getting Tom's pod to
change an API sequencing decision by writing a clear cost analysis, not by
authority.

**Priya** (still PM I at 16 months): scope evidence showed steady ownership
of her original feature area, not grown. Autonomy evidence showed strong
rung-1/2 decisions but no rung-3 instances — she still brings Amara-adjacent
calls to her Lead even when the Lead has told her she doesn't need to.
Influence evidence: none logged.

Amara's honest answer to Priya: "Dana's packet showed three things yours
doesn't yet — she grew her scope without being asked to, she made calls at
a rung above what was required of her, and she has one clear influence
story. None of that is about who I like more. Here's the gap, and here's
what closing it looks like in the next two quarters." Priya leaves with a
concrete target instead of a vague sense of unfairness — which is the
actual purpose of writing the ladder down.

## Common ladder-design failure modes

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Title inflation | Everyone becomes "Senior" within 2 years | Anchor levels to scope/autonomy/influence, review calibration yearly |
| Ladder as pure tenure clock | People expect promotion just for waiting | State explicitly: tenure is necessary, not sufficient |
| No lateral ceiling below Lead | Strong ICs feel forced into management to grow | Add a Staff/Principal PM track parallel to Product Lead |
| Different Leads apply different bars | Some pods promote faster than others | Cross-Lead calibration session every cycle, not each Lead deciding alone |

## Exercise

Draft one level of a career ladder for a role you're responsible for
developing — your own next level, or a level within your team. Fill in all
four components (scope, autonomy, influence, evidence) with specifics, not
adjectives. Then pick one person and write their evidence packet honestly,
including the gaps section — even if you never share it with them, writing
it will show you where your own promotion criteria are still vague.
